Independent Research | Understanding Power, Incentives, and Systems
I am an independent, curiosity-driven researcher interested in understanding how the world works beneath the surface of individual headlines.
My attention is drawn to the relationships between politics, economics, finance, technology, geopolitics, institutions, and public life. These subjects are often discussed separately, but real-world outcomes can emerge from the way they interact. I explore those intersections to understand not only what happened, but also why it happened, how it became possible, who may be affected, and what evidence supports the explanation.
Why I Do This
I research because complex events rarely become understandable through a single headline or an isolated fact. Policies can alter incentives; incentives can shape decisions; decisions can affect markets and communities; and relationships between institutions and individuals can influence how those decisions are made.
I want to look beyond the first explanation without automatically rejecting it. When something appears connected, I ask whether there is a credible mechanism linking the events—or whether the similarity is coincidental, the result of shared incentives, or better explained by ordinary institutional behaviour.
The goal is not to defend a predetermined theory. It is to develop a clearer, more complete understanding of the forces shaping events and to remain willing to revise my view when the evidence points elsewhere.
My Research Style
1. Start with questions, not conclusions
I treat an interesting pattern as a lead to investigate, not as proof. A hypothesis is useful when it helps identify what evidence to look for; it should never dictate what the evidence must say.
2. Follow connections across disciplines
I look for plausible links between political decisions, financial incentives, ownership, trade, energy, technology, institutions, and public outcomes. I pay attention to the mechanisms that could connect them rather than relying on timing or resemblance alone.
3. Build a chronology
Sequence matters. I establish what happened and when, then look backwards when earlier decisions, relationships, contracts, or structural conditions may help explain later events. I do not assume that the beginning of an investigation marks the beginning of the underlying story.
4. Separate evidence from interpretation
I aim to distinguish documented facts, attributed claims, reasonable inferences, open questions, and speculation. The strength of a conclusion should match the strength of the evidence supporting it.
5. Ask who benefits—and test the answer
Interests and incentives can reveal useful leads, but benefiting from an outcome does not prove that someone caused it. I look for evidence of the actual pathway between a decision, the people involved, and the resulting effect.
6. Consider competing explanations
I actively leave room for coincidence, independent actors responding to similar incentives, institutional constraints, unintended consequences, and explanations that do not involve coordination. A convincing investigation should test alternatives, not merely collect supporting examples.
7. Look for verifiable records
I value primary documents, official records, credible investigative journalism, financial disclosures, public statements, and sources that can be checked independently. Important claims should be traceable to their origins, and uncertainty should be stated plainly.
8. Follow the evidence wherever it leads
I do not begin by assuming that a single person, organisation, or hidden network controls events. If evidence supports a specific relationship or coordinated activity, it deserves careful examination. If it does not, that limit must be respected.
Questions That Guide My Work
What exactly happened, and what can be independently established?
What conditions and decisions made it possible?
Which people, institutions, and organisations had relevant authority, access, ownership, or incentives?
What mechanism could connect one event to another?
Who benefited, who bore the costs, and how can those effects be measured?
Is there evidence of a direct relationship or deliberate coordination, or only an apparent pattern?
What alternative explanations fit the same facts?
What new evidence would strengthen, weaken, or overturn the current interpretation?
What I Aim to Produce
I aim to turn scattered information into a structured, understandable account of events and their possible relationships. That means building timelines, mapping relevant actors and interests, checking sources, identifying gaps, testing explanations, and updating conclusions as new evidence emerges.
The emphasis is on understanding rather than sensationalism, evidence rather than assumption, and explanation rather than premature certainty.
This is an ongoing learning process. The objective is not to claim that every question has a hidden answer, but to ask better questions, recognise meaningful patterns when they are supported, and be equally prepared to conclude that a suspected connection has not been established.
Independent research. Open questions. Verifiable evidence. Conclusions proportional to what the evidence shows.
